# Charlie Munger — Core Framework (Working Notes)

These are source notes, not the final synthesis. Claims are provisional until checked across the full chronological corpus.

## Epistemology: build a lattice, then try to break it

- Facts become usable only when attached to theory. Munger's recurring unit of knowledge is not the isolated fact but a "latticework" of models that can organize direct and vicarious experience. He argues that a relatively small set—roughly 80–90 major models, with a handful doing most of the work—is sufficient if drawn from multiple disciplines. [Source: Munger, "A Lesson on Elementary, Worldly Wisdom," USC, 1994, https://worldlypartners.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/1994-lecture-by-charlie-munger-at-usc-a-lesson-on-elementary-worldly-wisdom-as-it-relates-to-investment.pdf]
- The requirement for multiple disciplines is defensive as much as additive: with only one or two models, the mind "will torture reality" to fit them. This is a warning against monocausal explanations and professional deformation, not merely a recommendation to read broadly. [Source: Munger, USC, 1994, same source]
- He ranks models by reliability: mathematics and hard-science/engineering models first, then biology/physiology, followed by the messier but indispensable psychology and microeconomics. Accounting is necessary as the language of business but is explicitly a "crude approximation" whose limitations must be understood. [Source: Munger, USC, 1994, same source]
- Munger uses a two-track analysis: (1) rationally evaluate the real interests and probabilities; (2) identify subconscious psychological forces that may be generating a wrong conclusion. This makes psychology a parallel audit of ostensibly rational analysis, not a replacement for it. [Source: Munger, USC, 1994, same source]
- Knowledge requires active use. In 2007 he says the big ideas must be learned deeply enough to enter a mental lattice and then practiced until their use becomes automatic; unused models decay. [Source: Munger, USC Gould commencement, 2007, https://worldlypartners.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/2007-commencement-speech-by-charlie-munger-at-usc-gould-school-of-law.pdf]
- Wisdom acquisition is framed as a moral duty and lifetime learning as its corollary. He describes Buffett's record as impossible without Buffett being a "continuous learning machine" and says progress depends more on what one learns after formal education than on what one already knows. [Source: Munger, USC Gould commencement, 2007, same source]
- Disconfirmation is a routine, not a temperament. Munger repeatedly invokes Darwin's practice of giving priority to evidence against his own favored ideas, and pairs this with inversion and checklist routines. The standard is active destruction-testing of beliefs, not passive openness to being wrong. [Source: Munger, Harvard School commencement, 1986, https://worldlypartners.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/1986-commencement-speech-by-charlie-munger-at-harvard-school-now-harvard-westlake.pdf; Munger, USC Gould commencement, 2007]
- By 1996-2003 he is pushing a stricter organizing norm than generic "read broadly": use the hard-science ethos of full attribution and reduction to more fundamental explanations where possible. He attacks disciplines that borrow useful ideas without labeling their source because that weakens the user's mental filing system. [Source: Munger, Stanford Law School lecture, 1996, https://worldlypartners.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/1996-lecture-by-charlie-munger-at-stanford-law-school-worldly-wisdom-revisited-business-what-lawyers-should-know.pdf; Munger, UCSB economics lecture, 2003, https://worldlypartners.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/2003-speech-by-charlie-munger-at-ucsb-academic-economics-strengths-and-faults-after-considering-interdisciplinary.pdf]
- His "iron prescription" against ideology is adversarial restatement: he is not entitled to an opinion until he can state the opposing case better than its supporters. [Source: Munger, USC Gould commencement, 2007, same source]
- Ideology is not just a political nuisance in this framework; it is a major cause of cognitive lock-in that can impair even geniuses. The 1996 Stanford lecture makes Buffett's ideological distance part of Buffett's cognitive edge. [Source: Munger, Stanford Law School lecture, 1996, same source]

## Inversion: prevention before optimization

- Inversion is presented as a general problem-solving operator: study how to create non-X, identify where failure or ruin occurs, and avoid those paths. The 1986 misery speech applies it to life; the 2007 address generalizes it to complex adaptive systems. [Source: Munger, Harvard School commencement, 1986; Munger, USC Gould commencement, 2007]
- The practical implication is asymmetric: eliminate unreliability, addiction, resentment, envy, ideology, self-pity, perverse incentives, and preventable ruin before seeking brilliance. A mediocre but reliable "turtle" can outrun gifted but unreliable people. [Source: Munger, Harvard School commencement, 1986, same source]
- Inversion does not mean pessimism. Munger explicitly describes anticipating trouble and preparing to perform adequately as compatible with a favored life. It is operational readiness, not emotional gloom. [Source: Munger, USC Gould commencement, 2007, same source]

## Psychology: incentives plus interacting tendencies

- The mature version of "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment" enumerates 25 tendencies: reward/punishment superresponse; liking/loving; disliking/hating; doubt avoidance; inconsistency avoidance; curiosity; Kantian fairness; envy/jealousy; reciprocation; influence from mere association; simple pain-avoiding psychological denial; excessive self-regard; overoptimism; deprival superreaction; social proof; contrast misreaction; stress influence; availability misweighing; use-it-or-lose-it; drug misinfluence; senescence misinfluence; authority misinfluence; twaddle; reason respecting; and lollapalooza combinations. [Source: Munger, "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment," revised version, https://worldlypartners.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/1995-speech-by-charlie-munger-at-harvard-the-psychology-of-human-misjudgment.pdf]
- The list is not intended as 25 independent labels. Munger's distinctive claim is combinatorial: multiple tendencies can reinforce one another into a "lollapalooza" outcome far larger than any component would predict. [Source: Munger, "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment," same source]
- Incentives occupy first position because rewards and punishments reshape both action and cognition. He repeatedly treats incentive-caused bias as a mechanism that makes sincere people rationalize conduct serving the reward system. [Source: Munger, "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment," same source]
- The Federal Express night-shift example illustrates mechanism design: hourly or exhortation-based management failed, while paying by completed shift aligned the reward with the actual system constraint and solved the problem. [Source: Munger, USC, 1994, same source]
- Persuasion should account for self-interest. Munger adopts Franklin's advice to appeal to interest rather than reason—not as cynicism, but because moral exhortation often loses to self-serving bias. [Source: Munger, USC Gould commencement, 2007, same source]
- The working unit is a checklist, not an anecdote bank. In the 1996 Stanford lecture he argues that psychologists and other professionals fail because they do not run complex cases through a full checklist of tendencies, especially when several forces combine in one direction. [Source: Munger, Stanford Law School lecture, 1996, same source]
- Several tendencies that become central in his later institutional criticism are already treated by 1996 as under-taught but essential: denial, envy, deprival superreaction, gambling/near-miss effects, and social proof under stress. [Source: Munger, Stanford Law School lecture, 1996, same source]

## Character and relationships: deserved trust as an operating asset

- The safest path to getting what one wants is to deserve it: deliver what one would buy if roles were reversed. Munger links this to reputation, respect, and "deserved trust," not simply commercial success. [Source: Munger, USC Gould commencement, 2007, same source]
- Reliability is a threshold trait. In the 1986 inversion speech, unreliability is sufficient to cancel other virtues; in 2007, sloth and unreliability are the easiest routes to failure. [Source: Munger, Harvard School commencement, 1986; Munger, USC Gould commencement, 2007]
- Choose close associations by admiration because authority and reward relationships alter behavior. Munger says he deliberately maneuvered to work under people he admired and wanted to emulate. [Source: Munger, USC Gould commencement, 2007, same source]
- The institutional ideal is a "seamless web of deserved trust": reliable people correctly trusting one another can outperform dense procedural control. This is not blind trust; the adjective "deserved" implies selection, track record, and aligned character. [Source: Munger, USC Gould commencement, 2007, same source]
- The Blue Chip letters show the concrete operating form of that principle before Munger used the later phrase so often. See's and Precision Steel are praised less for central supervision than for already possessing cultures of customer care and fair dealing, while Blue Chip's role is mainly intelligent selection plus non-interference. [Source: Blue Chip Stamps shareholder letters, 1978-1982, especially the 1978 "leave See's alone" passage and the 1979 Precision Steel discussion]
- The Wesco 2005-2007 meeting notes sharpen the operating meaning of that phrase. Berkshire's trust system is not just moral uplift; Munger says it creates "enormous efficiencies," allows very small headquarters, and depends on unusually careful selection plus tolerance for "subnormal process" rather than universal checking. [Source: Munger remarks in Whitney Tilson's 2005 and 2007 Wesco annual-meeting notes, third-party note reconstructions rather than official transcripts]
- His advice against envy, resentment, revenge, and self-pity is functional rather than sentimental: these modes distort cognition, prevent recovery, and add no constructive capacity after a setback. [Source: Munger, Harvard School commencement, 1986; Munger, USC Gould commencement, 2007]

## Investing: patience, quality, concentration, and price discipline

- Circle of competence is an edge constraint: determine where aptitude and developed knowledge create an advantage, play there, and accept that another person's rational opportunity may sit outside one's own circle. [Source: Munger, USC, 1994, same source]
- The betting model is pari-mutuel: competitive prices usually absorb common knowledge, but occasional material mispricings remain. Success therefore requires work, patience, and decisive concentration when rare high-confidence opportunities appear—not continuous prediction. [Source: Munger, USC, 1994, same source]
- Munger says most of Berkshire's wealth came from roughly ten major insights. Buffett's 20-punch-card thought experiment is intended to force selectivity, depth, and meaningful position sizing. [Source: Munger, USC, 1994, same source]
- Their break from Graham was the recognition that quantitative cheapness can understate a company's quality, competitive momentum, or exceptional system. The early "Geiger counter" bargains built the first hundreds of millions; the great majority of later wealth came from better businesses. [Source: Munger, USC, 1994, same source]
- The long-run return of a stock is tethered to the underlying return on capital of the business. A company earning 6% on capital for 40 years cannot usually produce a dramatically better shareholder return even if bought cheaply; a durable 18% compounder can succeed despite an apparently expensive initial price. [Source: Munger, USC, 1994, same source]
- Quality does not erase valuation. Munger immediately warns that obvious great companies became disastrous at Nifty-Fifty prices. The actual rule is a fairly priced great company, not a great company at any price. [Source: Munger, USC, 1994, same source]
- The Blue Chip letters are the operating-company version of that rule. See's appears as a wonderful business inside a lousy-to-mediocre industry, but the Buffalo Evening News shows the opposite lesson: buying a socially admirable or strategically understandable asset at a price/structure that leads to years of poor returns and huge opportunity cost can still leave owners worse off even if the strategic diagnosis was partly right. [Source: Blue Chip Stamps shareholder letters, 1977-1982, especially the 1981 opportunity-cost discussion]
- A great business with a great manager is ideal, but business economics usually deserve more weight than managerial brilliance. Rare exceptional managers can overcome mediocre starting conditions; on average, "bet on the business momentum." [Source: Munger, USC, 1994, same source]
- Inaction has mathematical value. Long holding periods reduce transaction friction, noise exposure, and tax drag; deferred taxation can add several percentage points to annual after-tax compounding. But tax avoidance itself can become a bias and produce poor investments. [Source: Munger, USC, 1994, same source]
- For permanent capital pools, concentration is not merely tolerated; it can be the rational answer. In 1998 Munger explicitly argues that some foundations or families can sensibly remain 90% concentrated in one great equity if their objective is long-run maximization rather than short-run career or prestige insurance. [Source: Munger, "Investment Practices of Leading Charitable Foundations," 1998, https://worldlypartners.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/1998-investment-practices-of-leading-charitable-foundations.pdf]
- Munger is not a neutral celebrant of liquid markets. In 1984 he argues that excess liquidity can turn stock markets into socially harmful casinos, misdirect talent, and intensify managerial short-termism; under some conditions he explicitly prefers less liquidity and harsher tax treatment for short-term gains. [Source: Munger, "Opinion Piece on the Stock Market" draft, 1984, https://worldlypartners.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/1984-opinion-piece-on-the-stock-market.pdf]
- The 2004-2007 Wesco meeting notes add an important late-cycle nuance: when opportunity sets are poor and scale is huge, Berkshire does not pretend old return standards still apply. Munger says they buy with "reduced expectations," lower standards somewhat rather than wait forever for 1974-style conditions, and still preserve the right to pounce when a rare big opportunity appears. [Source: Munger remarks in Whitney Tilson's 2005 and 2007 Wesco annual-meeting notes, third-party note reconstructions rather than official transcripts]
- The 2007 railroad discussion is a major anti-dogma example inside the investing framework. Munger says he and Buffett hated railroads for decades, then changed their minds when containerization, computing, and freight patterns altered the economics; the failure was not stubbornness forever, but being "too late smart." [Source: Munger remarks in Whitney Tilson's 2007 Wesco annual-meeting notes, third-party note reconstruction rather than an official transcript]

## Institutional design: structure determines behavior

- Berkshire's absence of redeemable clients was a strategic advantage: Buffett and Munger could tolerate path divergence, hold concentrated positions, and wait without being fired for looking different. [Source: Munger, USC, 1994, same source]
- Conventional investment management is shaped by agency incentives. Managers sell activity and diversification partly because a concentrated, low-turnover portfolio makes the fee visible and career risk acute. The system can be rational for the manager while harmful to the client. [Source: Munger, USC, 1994, same source]
- Specialization and scale can create powerful economic niches, but scale also breeds bureaucracy and perverse internal incentives. Munger consistently analyzes advantages and their embedded disadvantages together. [Source: Munger, USC, 1994, same source]
- The Blue Chip letters add a reporting-design dimension to this institutional view. Munger and Buffett treat shareholder communication itself as mechanism design: strip away accounting obscurity where possible, report what owners would actually want to know, and force explicit confrontation with opportunity cost and carrying-value distortions instead of hiding behind convention. [Source: Blue Chip Stamps shareholder letters, 1981-1982]
- He increasingly frames good institutions as systems that are hard to cheat. The design task is not just to reward virtue but to remove easy fraud, easy rationalization, and soft accountability; otherwise vice compounds through incentives, social proof, and habit. [Source: Munger, Stanford Law School lecture, 1996; Munger, UCSB economics lecture, 2003]
- Munger is more willing than many admirers admit to trade local fairness for system function. His favored examples include no-pay magistrates/directors, no-fault career penalties for Navy captains, and compensation systems that refuse claims categories when they are too gameable. [Source: Munger, Stanford Law School lecture, 1996; Munger, UCSB economics lecture, 2003]
- The same structure-first logic appears earlier in civic and market-regulation arguments. In 1984 he argues that takeover law and tax law should be changed to make socially wasteful financial games less attractive, even at the cost of some liquidity and some managerial exposure to market discipline. [Source: Munger, Statement to House Subcommittee, 1984, https://worldlypartners.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/1984-Charlie-Munger-Testimony.pdf; Munger, "Opinion Piece on the Stock Market" draft, 1984]
- His legitimacy test is harsher than generic efficiency analysis: markets should channel power and rewards toward people widely perceived as deserving, not toward intermediaries who mainly extract rents from churn, coercion, or accounting illusion. [Source: Munger, "Opinion Piece on the Stock Market" draft, 1984; Munger, Statement to House Subcommittee, 1984]
- By 1989 he is already treating loose accounting and undercapitalized financial institutions as a joint design failure, not separate moral lapses. The savings-and-loan resignation letter attacks trade-association lobbying, fake capital, and weak regulatory standards as interacting causes of disaster. [Source: Munger, "Resignation of Mutual Savings from U.S. League of Savings Institutions," 1989, https://worldlypartners.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/1989-resignation-of-mutual-savings-from-us-league-of-savings-institutions-letter-by-charlie.pdf]
- In the 2009-2011 crisis writings, this escalates into an overt constitutional program for capitalism: less tolerance for derivative bucket shops, less leverage, more conservative accounting, and willingness to use taxes or legal prohibitions to restore deserved trust in the system. [Source: Munger, "Sacrificing To Restore Market Confidence," 2009, https://worldlypartners.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/2009-sacrificing-to-restore-market-confidence.pdf; Munger, "Basically, It's Over," 2010, https://worldlypartners.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/2010-basically-its-over.pdf; Munger, "Wantmore, Tweakmore, Totalscum, and the Tragedy of Boneheadia," 2011, https://worldlypartners.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/2011-a-parody-about-the-great-recession.pdf]
- The 2014 Berkshire retrospective adds the positive institutional mirror image: tiny headquarters, extreme subsidiary autonomy, centralized capital allocation and CEO selection, heavy trust, cash acquisitions, low debt, and a leader who preserves large blocks of quiet reading/thinking time while setting cultural tone. [Source: Munger, "Vice Chairman's Thoughts - Past and Future," 2014, https://worldlypartners.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/2014-vice-chairmans-thoughts-past-and-future.pdf]
- Berkshire is not presented as a general management science template but as a system intentionally built around unusual human capital. Munger says Buffett designed it to maximize rationality, long-horizon consequences, loyalty, and anti-bureaucratic simplicity under conditions of extraordinary trust and skill. [Source: Munger, "Vice Chairman's Thoughts - Past and Future," 2014, same source]
- A subtle but important safeguard emerges here: Munger's preferred concentration of authority is never supposed to be generic. He wants power concentrated where judgment is extraordinary and incentives are clean, not merely wherever hierarchy already exists. [Inference from Munger's 2014 Berkshire essay and broader incentive doctrine]

## Provisional synthesis

Munger's system is best understood as a layered error-control architecture:

1. Start with character constraints: be reliable, deserve trust, avoid corrupting associations.
2. Build a multidisciplinary model lattice and keep learning.
3. Run rational analysis on interests, probabilities, and economics.
4. Run a separate psychological audit on incentives and biases.
5. Invert to identify failure, ruin, and disconfirming evidence.
6. Use checklists so knowledge is actually applied under pressure.
7. Wait inside the circle of competence.
8. Act decisively when quality, price, and odds align.
9. Preserve compounding through patience, low friction, and good institutional design.

This ordering is provisional and will be tested against the Wesco/Daily Journal chronology and Buffett's letters.
